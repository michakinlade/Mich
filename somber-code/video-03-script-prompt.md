# SCRIPT PROMPT: Video 3, "What Your Family Stops Telling You at Every Level of Wealth"

Copy everything below the line and paste it into any AI. To get a fresh variation, change the names, numbers and settings in the INPUTS block.

---

You are a scriptwriter for Somber Journal, a faceless YouTube channel that tells money stories in 2D animation. The channel is about how money quietly changes the way people treat you. It gives no financial advice and no how-to steps. Write ONE complete, original, voiceover-ready script.

## INPUTS
- Title: What Your Family Stops Telling You at Every Level of Wealth
- Format: Level Ladder, 7 levels
- Target length: 2,450 words (stay within ±5%), about 16 minutes at 150 wpm
- Levels: −$3,140 · $900 · $8,000 · $40,000 · $250,000 · $1.2 million · $10 million
- How the money grows: warehouse night shift → day-shift lead → a commercial cleaning side business → company sells a minority stake → full sale
- Cast (first names only; the protagonist is never named and never gendered):
  - Mom: calls every Sunday at 4:10 and always opens with "Are you eating?"
  - Marcus: older brother, a pharmacist, starts as the family success, later loses his job; fiancée Brielle
  - Aunt Denise: hosts Thanksgiving
  - Uncle Ray: asks "what are you doing for work now", later pitches business ideas
  - Cousin Tasha: the first person to ask you for money

## CORE IDEA
As your money rises, your family doesn't love you more or less. They change what they tell you. At the bottom they hide good news out of pity. At the top they hide their lives because they think you no longer need anything. The cost of wealth is being slowly left out of your own family.

## SAME TESTS AT EVERY LEVEL (re-run all of these at every level so the change is felt, not told)
1. Where you sit at Thanksgiving. It starts on a metal folding chair with one rubber foot missing, next to the kitchen door. Then you're handed a plate first, then moved to the long side of the table, then the dinner moves to your house, then you're at the head in an upholstered chair.
2. Mom's 4:10 Sunday call and how her opening line changes: "Are you eating?" → "Are you doing okay now?" → she rehearses before asking → "Is this a good time?" → she stops asking how you are.
3. The family group chat: you only react with emojis → you're tagged in fundraisers and "what do you think?" screenshots → you hear about events from photos.
4. Money requests: none → Tasha's $1,400 she never repays → the furnace → Ray's $60,000 partnership → nobody asks at all because small needs feel insulting.
5. Marcus: proud but doubting ("Just don't blow it this time") → hurt that Mom called you, not him → "I'm fine. Don't." after his layoff → "I don't know how to talk to you anymore."

## REQUIRED BEAT AT EVERY LEVEL: "What nobody tells you"
Just before each level's Rule line, add 1–2 sentences that reveal one thing the family kept from you, then name the silence with "At this level, silence is ___." Use this progression:
- −$3,140: Marcus got engaged months ago and nobody mentions good news around you. Silence is pity.
- $900: Marcus offered to co-sign a loan for you and Mom said "not yet". Silence is caution.
- $8,000: Ray called Mom to ask if you'd lend to him too. Silence is strategy.
- $40,000: Marcus's wedding was $11,000 short and his in-laws covered it. He'd rather owe them than you. Silence is pride.
- $250,000: there was a family dinner you weren't invited to, where they planned who would ask you for what. Silence is a plan.
- $1.2M: Marcus has been in therapy since the layoff and everyone knows but you. Silence is distance.
- $10M: nobody tells you anything because they think you need nothing. Silence is what they think respect looks like.

## LEVEL BLUEPRINT
- Level 1, −$3,140: a gas-station hot dog you call pasta, the folding chair, Ray's question and the second of silence that's "the whole verdict", leftovers pressed into your hands ("we know"). Thesis line within the first 250 words: families rank you by how much they worry about you, and worry looks like distance. Rule: "you're a warning they give the cousins."
- Level 2, $900: the first number without a minus sign, Mom adds the word "now", Denise hands you a plate first, Marcus's "just don't blow it". Rule: "a question they haven't finished asking."
- Level 3, $8,000: a raise to $21.40 an hour, Tasha's 11:52 p.m. text asking for $1,400, the good feeling of being "picked for a team", her beach photos in July, Ray announcing "management" to the table. Rule: "an option."
- Level 4, $40,000: the cleaning business takes off, Mom's $6,850 furnace ("I didn't want to bother your brother"), you finally tell her the truth that you're eating, Denise gives the folding chair away ("A family will never tell you that you've been promoted. They'll just move the furniture."). Rule: "the backup plan."
- Level 5, $250,000: a guest room that becomes the most expensive thing you own, Ray's "family helps family" pitch over your eggs. Include the moral discomfort: you like being asked, "it feels like oxygen". Marcus loses his job and texts "I'm fine. Don't." Rule: "the bank. And banks don't get phone calls on Sundays. They get requests."
- Level 6, $1.2M: Thanksgiving moves to your house without a vote, the upholstered chair at the head, and you wishing for the folding chair, meaning the person you were in it. Kids taught to speak to you politely. Marcus: "I don't know how to talk to you anymore." Mom: "Is this a good time?" Rule: "the story they tell at other people's tables."
- Level 7, $10M: Ray frames a business article about you, people ask about interest rates instead of you, the requests stop. A rented Smoky Mountains house where the old stories are told "like a eulogy". Tasha's daughter's graduation and Denise's surgery found out late. Final scene: Mom has saved you a foil-wrapped plate with masking tape that reads "For later. Eat." Rule: "a stranger they're proud of. And pride is what love turns into when it doesn't know what to say."

## OUTRO
"Somewhere tonight…" replays Level 1 in third person with a stranger in the folding chair: the rocking leg, the gravy squeeze, Ray's question, the passed rolls. Then close on one thesis line, in this spirit: "Your family doesn't love you more at every level. They don't love you less. They just slowly forget which version of you they're supposed to be loving."

## VOICE AND STYLE
- Second person, present tense, 100% of the time. The protagonist is never named. Supporting characters always get first names.
- The narrator is calm, intimate and slightly clinical. Warm toward people, never mocking the family: they are flawed, not villains.
- Name the missing or unexpected feeling, never the obvious one ("Not anger. Just arithmetic.").
- Mostly short sentences of 4–10 words and fragments. Every 5–7 lines, add one long cascading sentence of 25–40 words joined with "and…, and…, and…".
- Use negation runs ("Not X. Not Y. Just Z."). End most paragraphs on a 3–6 word punch.
- Numbers are always exact and slightly odd ($2.19, $6,850, 11:52 p.m.).
- Drop a quotable line every 150–230 words that could stand alone as a clip.
- Each level ends with the spoken verdict: "Rule [N]. At $[amount], you're not family. You're ___."
- Include at least one tender, unspoken-love moment and at least one moment where the protagonist notices something ugly in themselves.
- The ONLY direct question to the viewer is the closing comment question (see OUTPUT). No intro, no "welcome back", no channel name, no call to subscribe, no advice, no headings, no emojis, no scene directions.

## OUTPUT
1. First line: "TARGET: 2,450 words".
2. The full script as plain paragraphs, voiceover-ready.
3. After the final thesis line, end with this exact closing comment question as its own paragraph: "What's one thing your family stopped telling you when your money changed? Tell me in the comments. I read every one."
4. Last line: "FINAL: [N] words · est. runtime [N/150] min".

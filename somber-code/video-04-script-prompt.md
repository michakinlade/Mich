# SCRIPT PROMPT: Video 4, "What Losing Everything Looks Like, Level by Level"

Copy everything below the line and paste it into any AI. To get a fresh variation, change the names, numbers, business and settings in the INPUTS block.

Backup title if the main one is taken on YouTube: "From $3.8 Million to $0: What Leaves First".

---

You are a scriptwriter for Somber Journal, a faceless YouTube channel that tells money stories in 2D animation. The channel is about how money quietly changes the way people treat you. It gives no financial advice and no how-to steps. Write ONE complete, original, voiceover-ready script.

## INPUTS
- Title: What Losing Everything Looks Like, Level by Level
- Format: a Level Ladder that goes DOWN, 6 levels
- Target length: 2,450 words (stay within ±5%), about 16 minutes at 150 wpm
- Levels: $3.8 million · $900,000 · $210,000 · $38,000 · $4,600 · $0
- The protagonist (never named, never gendered) owns Kessler Freight: 42 trucks, 91 employees. One national home-goods retailer is 64% of revenue. A $1.4M line of credit is personally guaranteed.
- Cast (first names only):
  - Elena: spouse, a clinic worker, notices everything, says little
  - Nora: daughter, 11 then 12, built a water filter from a two-liter bottle and sand for a science fair; plays clarinet
  - Graham: the relationship banker
  - Hector: a driver since the second truck, laid off at Level 2
  - Brent: a friend from the club
  - Dad: 74, drives a 2004 Silverado with a tape deck
  - The host at Marlow's steakhouse, who knows your name

## CORE IDEA
Money doesn't leave all at once. It leaves in the order you stopped paying attention. As each thing goes, you learn who was calling the money and who was calling you. At zero, you find the one thing that was never on the balance sheet: your daughter, and the fact that you never listened to her.

## RECURRING MOTIFS (pay off every one)
1. The key ring. Level 1 has seven keys: office, warehouse, the house on Linden Court, the lake house, the boat, the Range Rover, and a brass key to a closet safe. Remove one or more on screen at each level. At the end, one cheap silver rental key is left, "cut so roughly it snags on your pocket lining".
2. Marlow's steakhouse. Level 1: the host says your name and your corner booth under the brass lamp has your wine already poured. Level 3: "a lovely table by the window". Level 4: a new host asks for your name and a reservation. You realise you never learned the old host's name.
3. Nora's water filter. Level 1: she describes it at dinner while you check a game score. Level 6: you ask her to tell you about it again.
4. Phone calls: plans become reviews, then pauses, then nothing. The calls that remain are the ones that were about you.

## LEVEL BLUEPRINT
- Level 1, $3.8M: the keys pressing into your leg ("the only part of the money you can actually touch"), Marlow's at 8:14 on a Thursday, calls offering plans not problems, the dangerous 64% client "because easy pays", Elena noticing you're not listening to Nora. Thesis within the first 250 words: "Money doesn't leave all at once. It leaves in the order you stopped paying attention." Rule: "you don't have money. You have momentum."
- Level 2, $900,000: a 6:02 a.m. email titled "Strategic Vendor Consolidation" with 90 days' notice, math in the shower, the guarantee you signed "like a birthday card". The boat is sold for $118,000 and its key comes off. You lay off 22 drivers yourself; Hector says "You'll be all right, boss." The glass-room review with Graham and an unnamed younger man, and "covenant ratio". Sitting in the garage listening to Nora's clarinet crack on the same note. Elena passes you salt you didn't ask for. Rule: "you didn't lose money. You lost the right to be careless."
- Level 3, $210,000: a bank letter with "regrettably", the lake house sold (keep the dock lights on a 7 p.m. timer that Nora used to watch), the window table at Marlow's, the golf trip going ahead without you. Brent offers a $450-an-hour consultant and asks about the club party with a telling pause ("There is always pressure in 'no pressure.'"). The $14,800 membership lapses and nobody calls. Elena in bed: "how bad is it?" Rule: "you're the person everyone's waiting to hear about."
- Level 4, $38,000: the company closes and the last trucks are hauled away with your name on the doors (callback to painting the first truck in your father's driveway with a cereal-box stencil). Office and warehouse keys come off. The Range Rover becomes a $7,300 Camry; Nora asks "Is it a rental?" and says "Oh." The new host at Marlow's. Dad offers his truck and talks about the weather for 20 minutes to keep you company. Hector at a gas station asks about everyone by name and never about money: "You still got my number, boss." Rule: "you find out who was calling the money, and who was calling you."
- Level 5, $4,600: Linden Court is sold and you move to an east-side rental with a ticking ceiling fan; Nora gets the bigger room. Two keys left. Job interviews where they wonder what went wrong with you. A grocery and bills breakdown where the $34-a-month clarinet rental is "the only one you'd fight for". You take a $58,000 dispatch job at the company that took your client. Elena at 1 a.m. with a calculator, scared alone for a year because you were too proud. Rule: "you didn't lose your money. You lost the story you told yourself about why you had it."
- Level 6, $0: the account reads exactly $0.00 on a Wednesday in November, "a zero with an echo". In the school drop-off line, Nora asks "Are we poor now?" Answer: "We don't have much money right now. That's different." Then: "Ask me about your science project… Because I wasn't listening. Tell me again." She talks for four minutes, the car behind honks, you don't move. Rule: "you find out what was never on the balance sheet. And it was the only thing that ever compounded."

## OUTRO
"Somewhere tonight, someone at $3.8 million is hanging a new key on a steel ring…" Replay Level 1 in third person: the brass lamp, a host who knows their name, a kid talking about something they built while the parent checks a score. Then: the keys are just doors, and the most expensive thing you own is the one you don't notice until it stops opening. Close on the single silver key and on the people behind that door "waiting for you, not for what you're carrying".

## VOICE AND STYLE
- Second person, present tense, 100% of the time. The protagonist is never named. Supporting characters always get first names.
- The narrator is calm, intimate and slightly clinical. Warm toward people, cold toward systems: banks never yell, they send letters.
- Name the missing or unexpected feeling, never the obvious one ("You notice that you notice.").
- Mostly short sentences of 4–10 words and fragments. Every 5–7 lines, add one long cascading sentence of 25–40 words joined with "and…, and…, and…".
- Use negation runs ("Not bankruptcy, not yet. Just closed."). End most paragraphs on a 3–6 word punch.
- Numbers are always exact and slightly odd ($118,000, $1,940 a month, 6:02 a.m.).
- Drop a quotable line every 150–230 words that could stand alone as a clip.
- Each level ends with a spoken verdict: "Rule [N]. At $[amount], ___."
- Include at least one tender, unspoken-love moment per level from Dad, Hector or Elena, and at least one moment where the protagonist sees their own pride as the problem.
- The ending is hopeful but earned: no rescue, no comeback money.
- The ONLY direct question to the viewer is the closing comment question (see OUTPUT). No intro, no "welcome back", no channel name, no call to subscribe, no advice, no headings, no emojis, no scene directions.

## OUTPUT
1. First line: "TARGET: 2,450 words".
2. The full script as plain paragraphs, voiceover-ready.
3. After the final thesis line, end with this exact closing comment question as its own paragraph: "What did losing money teach you that having it never did? Tell me below. I read every one."
4. Last line: "FINAL: [N] words · est. runtime [N/150] min".
